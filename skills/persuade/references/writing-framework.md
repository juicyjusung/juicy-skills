# Writing Framework: 3-Layer Sandwich Structure

## Overview

Every persuasion framework (OREO, PREP, PEEL, SDS) shares the same skeleton. This document codifies that shared structure into a single reusable pattern.

## The 3-Layer Sandwich

### Layer 1: Claim First (Top-Down)

State the conclusion up front. OREO's Opinion, PREP's Point, PEEL's Point, SDS's Summary — they all open with the takeaway. The reader should know "what this text is arguing" from the first sentence.

Why top-down matters: if the reader doesn't know the direction, even strong evidence slides past without registering.

### Layer 2: Evidence and Examples

Fill the middle with two components:

1. **Reason** — answers "Why?"
2. **Example/Evidence** — answers "For instance?"

Then add what PEEL calls the **Explain** step: after presenting the evidence, explicitly state *why this evidence supports the claim*. Without this connecting sentence, the text becomes a list of facts without a through-line.

Pattern for each piece of evidence:
```
[Reason] → [Example/Evidence] → [Explain: why this supports the claim]
```

Repeat for each piece of evidence (typically 2–3).

### Layer 3: Restatement

Close by restating the claim — but not as a copy-paste. The closing version should be stronger because it comes after the evidence. It's the same idea, earned by the argument.

Optionally, end with a concrete call to action.

## Counter-Argument Handling

Not part of the original frameworks, but essential for persuasion:

- Acknowledge 1–2 likely objections the reader would raise
- Provide a rebuttal for each
- Place this between the evidence section and the closing restatement

Ignoring obvious counter-arguments damages credibility — the reader thinks of them anyway and loses trust when they go unaddressed.

## Self-Check Principles

After drafting, verify these four things:

### 1. Direction Alignment
Does each piece of evidence actually support the claim?

A common failure: the evidence is true but irrelevant. "Exercise is good for health" supported by "the athletic shoe market is growing" — factually correct, logically disconnected. After writing each piece of evidence, ask: "Does this actually support my claim, or is it just related to the topic?"

### 2. No Evidence Conflicts
Do the pieces of evidence contradict each other?

Multiple pieces of evidence can individually support the claim but conflict with each other. "It saves money" followed by "the initial investment is large but worthwhile" confuses the reader about whether costs are low or high. Cross-check all evidence after laying them out.

### 3. Example Proof Value
Does the example actually prove the evidence it's attached to?

Anecdotes that are related but don't prove the point are a common trap. Statistics, research results, and concrete numbers carry more weight than vague stories.

### 4. Counter-Argument Coverage
Are obvious objections addressed?

If a reader can immediately think of a counter-argument that the text ignores, the entire argument loses credibility. Better to acknowledge and rebut than to pretend it doesn't exist.

## Practical Sequence (for drafting)

1. Write the claim in one sentence — this becomes the first and last sentence's prototype
2. Ask "Why?" and extract 2–3 reasons; check they don't conflict with each other
3. For each reason, ask "For instance?" and attach a concrete example or data point
4. For each example, write one sentence explaining why it supports the reason (the Explain step)
5. Think of at least one counter-argument and prepare a rebuttal
6. Restate the claim in a stronger form earned by the evidence, or propose a specific action

The result: structurally follows OREO/PREP, analytically reaches PEEL depth, and handles objections that the basic frameworks leave out.
